# Broiler chick hatch-to-first-feed/water — EU/UK, reviewed 2026-10-11

**Status:** draft; independent review needed  
**One-line summary:** Conventional hatchery workflows commonly leave chicks without feed and water until farm placement, often 24–48 hours after hatch. Reducing this deprivation is a plausible welfare improvement, especially for early-hatching chicks, but the welfare effects and best implementation vary and should not be inferred from growth alone.

## Scope
Broiler chicks from emergence from the shell through placement and early rearing in EU/UK supply chains. Relevant exposure is time from each chick's own hatch to first effective access to water and feed, not merely time from hatchery pull or arrival at the farm.

## Proposed causal pathway
Providing accessible water and feed sooner should reduce thirst and hunger, dehydration and reliance on yolk reserves. Earlier access may also affect early development. Delivery systems must actually enable access for chicks of differing hatch times and condition; nominal availability is not equivalent to consumption. On-farm hatching may avoid transport before placement but changes the hatching/rearing environment and must be assessed as a system, not as a single input.

## Expected impact and uncertainty
- **Animals affected:** potentially all hatchery-hatched broilers; quantify by supplier and include each individual's hatch-to-access distribution, not only flock averages.
- **Welfare change:** likely reduction in duration of hunger/thirst when access is genuinely earlier. Severity and persistence are not adequately quantified here; distinguish water from feed access and actual intake from provision.
- **Confidence:** high that conventional logistics create substantial delays for many chicks; moderate that access during/soon after hatch reduces deprivation; lower on magnitude of lifetime welfare effects and system-wide net benefit.
- **Evidence gaps:** direct validated indicators of hunger/thirst and affect; distribution of time-to-access; mortality/culling; effects by breed, hatch window and transport; comparative impacts of in-hatcher feeding, moist feed, and on-farm hatching; implementation and biosecurity constraints.

## Risks, trade-offs, and alternatives
Do not equate improved growth, organ development, or feed conversion with improved experienced welfare without a validated causal link. Compare interventions on chick welfare and mortality, handling/transport, thermal/light conditions, access and usability, and effects on workers and other animals. Include chicks that die or are culled; survivor-only assessments can conceal harms. Changes that shift hatching or rearing between sites need supply-chain and displacement assessment.

## Conclusion
Reducing hatch-to-first-water/feed delay is a defensible welfare objective, particularly for early hatchers, but claims about lifetime benefit or a preferred technology need context-specific evidence. Record hatch-to-access distributions and animal-centered outcomes, including mortality, before and after implementation. Do not treat a standard maximum delay as evidence that all chicks' needs are met.
