# Which pain-mitigation protocols reduce piglet castration pain? — EU/UK, draft, 2026-10-10

**Status:** draft for source review. **Summary:** Surgical castration causes acute pain and can cause pain-related behaviour during recovery. Studies support phase-specific benefits from local anaesthesia and postoperative NSAID analgesia, but protocols and outcomes differ; no single protocol is established as best across the whole peri-operative period. Pain mitigation is not an argument for castration itself: EFSA prefers immunocastration, with keeping males entire as another alternative.

## Scope
- **Population/context:** male piglets around 3–7 days old in commercial production, from handling and drug/injection through surgery and short-term recovery (minutes to approximately 24 h; longer follow-up when a source provides it). Do not generalize neonatal results to older animals or other castration techniques.
- **Geography and decision:** EU and UK producers and herd veterinarians choosing an evidence-informed peri-operative protocol if surgery continues. National law and medicines authorization are separate decisions and must be checked for the specific jurisdiction/product.
- **Question:** Which combination and timing of local anaesthesia and systemic analgesia reduces pain during injection/incision/traction and after surgery, and what outcomes remain uncertain?
- **Not a case for surgery:** EFSA recommends surgical castration without both anaesthesia and analgesia should not be performed, names immunocastration as the preferred alternative, and keeping males entire as the next-best alternative. These alternatives are context only; this note does not rank them against each other or against surgery. [EFSA 2022](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2022.7421), [plain-language summary](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/plain-language-summary/welfare-pigs-farm).

## Proposed causal pathway and terminology
Local anaesthesia blocks nociceptive signalling from the operative site while it is effective; an NSAID such as meloxicam is an analgesic intended to reduce inflammatory/postoperative pain, not to render the piglet unconscious. General anaesthesia suppresses consciousness during the operation, but alone does not establish adequate postoperative pain relief. Injection of an anaesthetic is itself a handling/injection event and may be noxious. Therefore protocols should be assessed separately for the injection, skin incision, testicular traction/cord cutting, and postoperative period; combining modalities is biologically plausible but not a proven universal winner. Drug use, dose, timing and authorization require veterinary oversight.

## Evidence
| Claim / phase | Evidence and source (study type) | Strength / limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Castration produces acute pain-associated behavioural changes; tested regimens did not yield a clear overall protocol winner. | Yun et al. (2019), randomized controlled behavioural trial, 143 male piglets from 29 sows; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31125820/), DOI [10.1016/j.rvsc.2019.05.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rvsc.2019.05.009). | Treatments included no castration, surgery without medication, preoperative meloxicam, intratesticular lidocaine, or isoflurane plus meloxicam. Behaviour was recorded around surgery (immediately, 1 and 2 h) and twice the following day. Untreated castrated piglets changed several behaviours immediately after surgery, supporting pain. Some single behaviours differed between treatment groups, but authors concluded efficacy of the tested protocols shortly after castration was not verified. Behavioural outcomes and follow-up were limited; do not infer a robust negative effect for all anaesthetic/analgesic protocols. |
| Local anaesthetic reduced acute nociceptive responses during surgery under a controlled anaesthesia model; injection itself and field transfer are important limits. | Saller et al. (2020), prospective randomized double-blinded physiological/limb-movement experiment, 54 piglets aged 3–7 days; [PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0236742), [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7392247/). | Four local agents (procaine, lidocaine, bupivacaine, mepivacaine) were compared with saline and handling controls. **All piglets received minimum-alveolar-concentration isoflurane** to reduce fear/stress as confounders; this is a laboratory measurement model, not a feasible/validated farm protocol. Local agents reduced some blood-pressure/heart-rate changes and limb movements associated with castration versus saline. Injection responses were measured too. The anaesthetic background means results cannot alone show the effect or practicality of these local agents in awake piglets; small groups and acute physiologic proxies limit translation. |
| Topical local anaesthetic applied after incision reduced responses to subsequent surgical stimuli and pain-related behaviour in the first 30 min, but not pain of the initial incision or later recovery. | Sheil et al. (2021), randomized commercial field trial on two European farms; 173 enrolled (86 treated, 87 untreated), topical wound anaesthetic instilled immediately after skin incision with a 30-s wait; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34679785/), [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8532673/), DOI [10.3390/ani11102763](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11102763). | Treated piglets had reduced motor/vocal nociceptive responses during subsequent castration and lower pain-related behaviour over the first 30 min post-castration. The intervention cannot prevent pain from the preceding incision; the short window does not establish duration of benefit over hours or a full recovery period. The product and protocol are not interchangeable with injected local anaesthetics or NSAIDs; check local product authorization. |
| Separate local anaesthetic and NSAID effects may address intraoperative and postoperative phases, respectively. | Hansson et al. (2011), randomized within-litter field study across five Swedish herds (four piglets in each of 141 litters); [Acta Vet Scand](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3123560/), DOI [10.1186/1751-0147-53-34](https://doi.org/10.1186/1751-0147-53-34). | Lidocaine given ≥3 min before castration was associated with lower-intensity calls and fewer resistance movements during surgery. Meloxicam given after castration was associated with fewer pain-related behaviours the following day; no treatment differences in mortality, growth or sickness treatments were detected through 3 weeks. Large field sample and real-herd setting help relevance, but technicians were not blinded, some herds used different castration techniques, treatment protocol/outcomes differed by phase, and control wounds were less swollen; these complicate a simple “combination wins” conclusion. |

### What studies measure
Vocalization, limb/resistance movement and autonomic responses during tissue manipulation are acute nociception indicators, not a complete measure of the piglet’s experience. Posture/activity and specific pain-associated behaviours after surgery address recovery but are brief observations and may miss subtler or later effects. Wound swelling, growth and mortality are relevant safety/health outcomes but cannot alone establish pain relief. Distinguish significant changes in one indicator from a demonstrated clinically meaningful improvement across phases; report untreated/sham contrasts and null outcomes.

## Regulatory context (checked 2026-10-10; not legal advice)
Legislation sets minimum permissions/requirements, not proof that a permitted procedure is pain-free or that a tested product is authorized for this use. The EU directive and domestic UK rules are not interchangeable; UK jurisdictions have separate implementing instruments and codes. In the EU, Council Directive 2008/120/EC Annex I, Chapter I, paragraph 8 permits castration only by means that do not involve tearing tissue and requires that if performed **after the seventh day of life**, it be under anaesthetic plus additional prolonged analgesia by a veterinarian ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32008L0120)).

For the UK, age wording and legal instruments differ: England’s permitted-procedures regulations require anaesthetic and additional prolonged analgesia where the pig is aged 7 days or over ([SI 2007/1100, Schedule 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2007/1100/pdfs/uksi_20071100_en.pdf)). Separately, the England Code of Practice says keepers should consider whether castration is necessary, avoid it wherever possible, and that analgesia is advisable for piglets younger than 7 days too (para. 151); this is code guidance, not the same as the statutory minimum ([current England Code, updated 5 October 2023](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pigs-on-farm-welfare/caring-for-pigs)); Wales has a corresponding requirement in its permitted-procedures regulations ([SI 2007/1029, Schedule 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/wsi/2007/1029/pdfs/wsi_20071029_mi.pdf)); Scottish government guidance specifies anaesthetic and additional prolonged analgesia for pigs older than 7 days and veterinary performance ([gov.scot guidance](https://www.gov.scot/publications/guidance-welfare-pigs/pages/14/)); Northern Ireland’s permitted-procedure rules allow lay castration only up to 7 days, without tearing tissues ([SR 2012/153, Schedule 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisr/2012/153/2012-04-02)), with the wider farmed-animal requirements in [SR 2012/156](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisr/2012/156/contents). Verify the full local rule, age calculation, operator requirement and current amendments with a qualified veterinarian/authority before relying on this brief comparison. Codes provide guidance and are not themselves the same as legislation.

None of these rules establishes approval of a specific local anaesthetic, dose, route or topical preparation for piglet castration. Medicines use, prescription, withdrawal periods and product authorisation vary; this note does not give dosing instructions. The protocol evidence is not a substitute for current veterinary medicines advice.

## Expected impact and uncertainty
The evidence is sufficient for a useful decision note about phase-specific protocol components, not a reliable head-to-head ranking. Local anaesthesia has support for acute intraoperative pain reduction in some designs; NSAIDs have support for some postoperative measures, but results differ by timing and study. The strongest limitation is generalizability: Saller’s animals were all lightly anesthetized; the short field trial of topical anesthetic captures mainly the immediate post-procedure window; Yun did not verify the efficacy of tested protocols shortly after surgery. Few trials use comparable outcome measures or test a consistent multimodal protocol through prolonged recovery. Incidence/severity and duration of pain avoided per piglet cannot be reliably estimated from these heterogeneous studies.

Confidence is moderate that castration itself is painful and low-to-moderate that certain anesthetic/analgesic components reduce measured pain indicators in particular windows. Confidence is low for a best-performing, universally applicable protocol or long-term recovery effect. Future decision-changing evidence would include adequately powered field trials comparing feasible multimodal protocols, measuring injection pain, incision/traction pain, and recovery over at least 24–48 h, with predefined behavioral and physiological outcomes, wound complications, safety and practical implementation.

## Risks, trade-offs, and alternatives
- Do not imply mitigation makes routine surgical castration necessary, ethically neutral, or pain-free. Consider immunocastration or raising entire males as alternatives in producer decisions; this note does not assess their relative welfare, husbandry or market consequences.
- Injection may itself cause pain and adds restraint/handling; a protocol may reduce one phase while leaving another untreated. General anesthesia is not equivalent to analgesia during recovery.
- Avoid extrapolating a veterinary experimental protocol or product-specific trial to another formulation, dose, age, route, nation or authorization status.
- Check for adverse events, wound healing, piglet nursing/activity, sow-piglet interactions, duration of recovery and labor/handling burden, not only immediate vocal/motor responses.

## Conclusion
Where surgery continues, evidence supports addressing both intraoperative nociception and postoperative pain, with local anaesthesia and systemic analgesia as distinct components rather than interchangeable treatments. Several trials report phase-specific benefits, but the protocols are not directly comparable and the best combination is uncertain. Follow applicable jurisdiction-specific law and veterinary medicines requirements; monitor both the procedure and recovery. These findings do not justify castration over available alternatives.
