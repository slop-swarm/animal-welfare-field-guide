# Indoor housing and laying-hen welfare — EU/UK evidence (draft)

**Status:** draft for review, 2026-10-10. **Summary:** Non-cage housing generally enables more movement and comfort/exploratory behaviour than cages, but has distinct risks, including collision-related keel damage. “Cage-free” is not itself a welfare outcome; design, rearing, management and measured animal outcomes matter.

## Scope
Laying hens during production in commercial indoor furnished/enriched cage and non-cage systems (single-/multi-tier) in EU/UK. Conventional battery cages appear only as a historical/reference comparator in older UK studies; they have been prohibited in the EU since 2012 and are not a current legal EU production choice. Excludes outdoor/free-range and organic systems. This is a concise evidence note, not a systematic review.

## Evidence and causal pathways
| Claim | Evidence | Strength / limits |
|---|---|---|
| Cages constrain movement and some comfort, exploratory and foraging behaviours; non-cage systems can enable these more often. | EFSA AHAW Panel, *Welfare of laying hens on farm* (2023), https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.7789 | EU scientific opinion synthesizing literature, expert assessment and modelling. EFSA ranks these welfare consequences as more relevant in cages and slightly relevant in non-cage systems. Systems/resources vary; this is not proof of good welfare in every non-cage flock. |
| Injuries are not a simple cage/non-cage binary. Keel bone lesions occur in all systems; navigation/collisions and furniture layout may affect risk. | EFSA (2023), above; Sherwin, Richards & Nicol, UK comparative study (2010), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20924842/ | EFSA rates bone lesions highly relevant in all housing. The PubMed abstract reports results from 26 flocks (6 conventional-cage, 6 furnished-cage, 7 barn, 7 free-range): conventional-cage birds had more fractures at depopulation; barns had the highest prevalence of several listed problems; and furnished-cage birds had the fewest problems in this sample. This is a small observational study; potential confounding and limited generalizability preclude treating it as a universal system ranking. |
| Mortality varies within and between systems and is not a sufficient summary of welfare. A 16-country commercial-flock meta-analysis found mortality in cage-free aviaries declined with accumulated system experience; it reported no significant cage vs cage-free mortality difference in more recent years. | Schuck-Paim, Negro-Calduch & Alonso (2021), 6,040 flocks/~176m hens, 2000–2019, *Scientific Reports*, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-81868-3; EFSA (2023) summarizes this finding. | Observational pooled analysis, not EU/UK-specific or proof that housing causes the trend. Results depend on study coverage and production-system maturity; management, flock and regional variation remain important. Consider causes/timing and culling, not only deaths among survivors. |
| Welfare monitoring should include birds lost before slaughter and use comparable measures. | EFSA (2023), above | EFSA identifies mortality, plumage damage, wounds, keel fractures and carcass condemnations as promising measures, while noting inconsistent methods/limited validation; slaughterhouse data miss hens that died or were culled on farm. |

## Expected impact and uncertainty
Housing affects hens throughout the laying cycle. Non-cage space, litter and elevated structures can support motivated behaviours; benefits depend on actual access and usability. In any system, poor litter, pecking, disease, collisions and management failures can cause serious harm. Keel fractures may be painful and impair movement. Report outcomes per flock/cycle, with definitions and denominators; do not multiply uncertain prevalence by speculative severity or assume one measure captures experience.

Confidence is moderate that cages restrict some motivated behaviours and that outcomes vary substantially with design/management; low for a single numerical net-welfare ranking across farms. The strongest directly scoped UK comparison is small and older. EFSA synthesizes EU evidence but across heterogeneous systems and study types; the large mortality analysis covers 16 countries, not EU/UK alone. Include on-farm deaths and culls as well as survivor outcomes.

## Risks, trade-offs and alternatives
- A label or transition does not guarantee realized improvement. Monitor measured flock outcomes, not promises alone.
- Track mortality and culling over the full cycle, causes if known, keel damage (prevalence and severity), plumage/wounds, and access/use of litter and perches, using consistent protocols.
- Do not assume more behavioural opportunity offsets painful injury or death, nor that a lower injury rate offsets severe movement restriction; make welfare trade-offs explicit.
- Record where displaced production/animals go, substitute systems and outcomes, and additionality/durability.
- Compare specific design and management practices (pullet rearing, genetics, nutrition, light, litter, stocking, handling), not just broad housing labels.

## Conclusion
Evidence supports a behavioural advantage of non-cage housing over cages, while documenting serious problems in both. Current comparisons do not establish a context-free net-welfare winner. A transition away from cages should be paired with monitoring and design/management to reduce fractures, pecking and mortality while enabling hens to use space and resources. Update as standardized longitudinal EU/UK flock data emerge.
