# Reducing chronic hunger in broiler breeders — EU/UK, reviewed 2026-10-10

**Status:** draft for review  
**One-line summary:** Broiler-breeder feed restriction is a widespread, prolonged welfare problem; evidence indicates some dietary and genetic/management changes can reduce hunger-related indicators, but no approach is established as eliminating hunger while reliably preserving health and reproductive output in commercial EU/UK conditions.

## Scope
- Species/population: female broiler-breeder pullets and hens; male breeders included where evidence is directly relevant.
- Geography/time: EU/UK commercial breeder systems; evidence includes controlled North American studies and must not be treated as a direct local forecast.
- Lifetime window: rearing (roughly first weeks through sexual maturity) and, where data permit, laying period.
- Decision-maker: breeder companies and integrated producers choosing breeder genotype and feeding standards.
- Decision: which feeding/genotype options to prefer to reduce chronic hunger while maintaining acceptable health and reproductive outcomes.

This note is distinct from the guide's broiler grow-out growth-rate procurement note: it concerns parent-stock birds kept to reproductive maturity, not the meat birds they produce. Legal requirements are not summarized here pending a jurisdiction-specific review; this note does not infer that welfare evidence itself changes what is legally permitted.

## Proposed causal pathway
Fast-growth broiler genetics confer high appetite and growth potential in parent stock. Feed is quantitatively restricted to limit excessive growth and body condition, which supports health and reproduction but causes prolonged hunger and frustrated feeding motivation. More satiating/less energy-dense diets, longer feeding access, feeding presentation that provides foraging and reduces unequal feeder access, less restrictive genotypes, or combinations might reduce hunger indicators. If growth/energy restriction is relaxed too far, excessive body weight can harm mobility, health, fertility, and egg production; some alternative diets or schedules can also create distinct harms.

## Evidence
| Claim / outcome | Evidence and source | Strength / limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Feed restriction causes prolonged hunger in broiler breeders; severity is generally greatest during rearing, and no known mitigation fully prevents it. | EFSA AHAW Panel, 2023, [Welfare of broilers on farm](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.7788), esp. breeder feed-restriction conclusions. | Systematic expert synthesis; not a primary trial. EFSA identifies direct/indirect animal-based measures, but commercial prevalence and magnitude are incompletely measured. It notes competition can prevent some birds obtaining the ration and recommends enough feeder space to allow simultaneous feeding; this may mitigate unequal access, not chronic hunger itself. |
| A rationed alternative diet and two non-daily schedules were compared against daily control in 1,680 Ross 308 pullets across 24 pens under simulated commercial conditions. Alternative treatments had lower feeding motivation during early rearing and better feather coverage/fewer fault bars; the study reported no overall performance penalty during rearing. | Arrazola et al. (2019), [rearing study](https://doi.org/10.3382/ps/pez170). | Randomized controlled, but one genotype and research setting. Treatments combined dietary and feeding-frequency differences; physiological and behavioral measures are proxies, not direct subjective hunger reports. Non-daily schedules entail off-feed days and should not be assumed benign. |
| Rearing treatment effects can persist into lay and outcomes are mixed. In the same cohort, graduated feeding was associated with higher cumulative egg production by 64 weeks than control; non-daily-fed birds laid lighter eggs, among other differences. | Arrazola et al. (2019), [lay study](https://doi.org/10.3382/ps/pez447). | Controlled follow-up, same experimental population rather than independent replication. Results are treatment- and age-dependent; differences in productivity do not by themselves establish welfare benefit. |
| Slower/intermediate-growing breeder strains may require less severe restriction during rearing; in one study, strains with lower restriction had lower signs of feeding frustration, better uniformity, and fewer footpad lesions/fault bars. | Arrazola & Torrey (2021), [slower-growing breeders during rearing](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2021.101434). | Controlled study of 360 birds across five strains in 20 pens; each strain in four pens. Sex/life-stage and strain matter, and results do not isolate genotype from all management/biological differences or predict EU/UK scale. Slower-growing birds still may require some restriction. |
| Diet dilution and roughage can alter clinical and stress indicators. | Tahamtani et al. (2020), [qualitative feed restriction study](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2020.00316). | Controlled study (1,200 Ross 308 females, 24 pens). The 19-week study found improved indicators in birds receiving maize silage roughage, poorer welfare indicators in the mixed soluble/insoluble fibre treatment, and little difference between insoluble-oat-hull and control birds on clinical outcomes. All treatments were scatter-fed and growth-matched. Mortality and plasma corticosterone did not differ statistically between treatments, though a control pen was culled after persistent cannibalism and excluded from the mortality analysis. These mixed results concern specific formulations, and do not establish commercial-scale effectiveness or generality to other genotypes. |
| A high-insoluble-fibre diet with roughage improved selected welfare indicators in fast-growing pullets; body-weight uniformity, litter quality, and mortality did not differ from standard diet. | Wurtz et al. (2025), [high fibre and roughage study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2025.105960). | Controlled study of 600 Ross 308 females in 12 pens (two treatments). Better plumage quality, hyperkeratosis, hock-burn scores, dirtiness, and scapular fault-bar outcomes in the experimental treatment, but null results for several health/production measures. Single commercial genotype and limited pen-level replication; no direct estimate of hunger reduction or commercial implementation. |
| Slower-growing breeder genotypes may require less restriction and show welfare/productivity differences. | Arrazola et al. (2022), [slower-growing breeders during lay](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2022.101917). | Controlled Canadian study of 336 birds from five intermediate/slower-growing strains in 12 pens during lay. Some groups had lower feeding motivation and favorable physical indicators with good reproductive output; reported mortality was below 2% for hens and 1% for roosters, but strain-specific reproductive problems occurred and all strains remained feed restricted. The small number of pens, one company’s genotypes, and controlled setting preclude a direct estimate of EU/UK adoption or an all-genotype guarantee. |

EFSA's assessment distinguishes strategies: insoluble-fibre dilution and reduced protein within balanced amino-acid requirements can reduce hunger indicators; soluble fibres may create discomfort/wet litter; calcium propionate can induce negative affect; scattered/mash feed may extend feeding time; evidence for skip-a-day schedules is controversial and health/production effects may be adverse. These are not interchangeable interventions, and combinations may perform differently from single measures.

### Welfare outcomes and proxies
The strongest directly decision-relevant outcomes include prolonged hunger (an affective welfare consequence inferred through combinations of animal-based indicators), stereotypic/abnormal feeding-related behaviour, competition and unequal access to the daily ration, feeding-time distribution, injurious pecking, mobility/lameness, health, and mortality. Feeding motivation tests, corticosterone, feather condition, growth trajectory, and water-seeking are indirect indicators with limitations; they should not be collapsed into one generic welfare score. Fertility, hatchability, egg numbers, and body weight are production/health outcomes relevant to feasibility and trade-offs, not standalone measures of welfare.

## Expected impact and uncertainty
- **Animals affected:** potentially all female and male parent-stock birds in systems using restriction; local numbers and current feeding schedules require up-to-date EU/UK data. No estimate is made here.
- **Magnitude/duration:** hunger can recur daily across much of rearing and may continue, though often less severely, into lay. EFSA describes restriction in rearing as potentially reducing feed to roughly 20–25% of ad-libitum intake for some systems; this is not a universal value.
- **Adoption/persistence:** depends on breeder/integrator standards, feed availability and cost, genotype supply, fertility/hatchability, and farm implementation. Evidence on real-world adoption and compliance is sparse.
- **Confidence:** high that conventional restriction produces prolonged hunger; moderate that some specific dietary/feeding interventions improve selected indicators in controlled trials; lower for sustained commercial EU/UK effect, comparative net welfare, and reproductive/health trade-offs across genotypes.
- **Key unknowns:** commercial-farm prevalence and severity; robust longitudinal comparisons across current genotypes; impacts on mortality, lameness, injurious pecking, fertility and hatchability; cost and scalability; sex- and stage-specific effects (especially in males); feeding access competition under different feeder/presentation systems; whether combined mitigations reduce actual duration/severity of hunger.

## Risks, trade-offs, and alternatives
Avoid extrapolating from small or controlled trials to flock-level outcomes. Increased feed availability can lead to excess body condition, lameness, reproductive dysfunction, and mortality if growth is not controlled. Alternative high-fibre formulations may affect litter, gut comfort, nutrient adequacy, or feed handling. Skip-a-day schedules may increase hunger on off-feed days and can impair health or production. A production shift to different breeder genotypes could alter offspring growth, market outcomes, and use of other breeds; those trade-offs should be measured rather than assumed. Feed restriction may partly be maintained for cost reasons as well as biological requirements.

## Conclusion
The evidence supports reducing the severity of restriction and testing combinations of lower-restriction genotypes, insoluble-fibre dilution, balanced lower-protein diets, and feeding presentation/frequency that give birds more opportunity to eat/forage, while monitoring welfare and reproductive outcomes together. It does not support a claim that any one current option eliminates hunger or is universally superior. Before recommending a specific standard, decision-makers need replicated longitudinal commercial trials with direct behavioral/health outcomes, production outcomes, and mortality reported by genotype and sex.
