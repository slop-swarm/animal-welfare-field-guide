# Hatch-to-first-feed and water for broiler chicks — EU/UK (draft)

**Status:** Evidence note in progress; not an endorsement of any system.  
**Decision question:** Which early-life arrangements, if any, reduce time without feed and water for broiler chicks and improve welfare, without unacceptable trade-offs? This note concerns grow-out chicks, not broiler-breeder feeding or grow-out genetics.

## Short answer
Conventional hatchery hatching followed by handling, transport, and farm placement can leave early-hatching chicks without access to feed and water for many hours. EFSA identifies this as a welfare concern, but direct evidence that any one commercial alternative improves whole-life welfare is limited. On-farm hatching removes chick transport and can provide immediate access to resources, but entails egg transport/altered farm operations and has mixed findings. Hatchery feed/water, wet feed at placement, and transport feeding are separate interventions and should not be treated as interchangeable. A controlled Northern Ireland comparison found few consistent benefits for hatchery feed/water or wet feed; in-house hatching showed some benefits and some negative findings. Do not infer that early growth or yolk-sac changes alone establish improved subjective welfare.

## Why the question matters
In conventional systems chicks hatch over a window, are processed and transported, and generally first encounter feed and water at the grow-out farm. The resulting deprivation interval varies between chicks; flock-average time from hatch is therefore an incomplete measure. EFSA’s 2023 scientific assessment concludes that a delay of more than 48 h (range 36–60 h) from hatch to first access to feed and water may lead to prolonged hunger and thirst. It recommends hatchery feeding or on-farm hatching, and keeping hatch-to-placement time as short as possible and no more than 48 h. These are expert scientific recommendations, not themselves legislation.

## Evidence by intervention

### On-farm hatching
A commercial-scale controlled Northern Ireland study randomly assigned eggs from one parent flock among five early-life treatments, with six replicate groups per treatment and around 500 birds/group. The on-farm-hatched treatment avoided transporting newly hatched chicks and gave chicks access to feed and water after hatch. Compared with conventional hatchery practice, the authors reported some potential benefits, including more resting and greater bursa weight, but also worse numerical chick-quality scores; there were no consistent improvements across behavioral, gait, litter, or performance outcomes. Overall mortality was higher for hatchery water and hatchery feed+water groups than conventional control, but not distinguishable from the on-farm group. A single cycle and limited replication of treatment at pen level constrain inference; the study does not establish a general mortality effect or a net welfare winner. Authors disclosed industry funding/participation.

On-farm hatching may reduce chick handling, transport, and the post-hatch interval without resources, but requires moving eggs late in incubation and managing hatchery functions in a poultry house. Potential biosecurity, environmental-control, labor, capital, chick-quality, and hatch-window issues need independent evaluation; do not assume these are net harms or benefits without evidence. The studied protocol still included counting/grading after hatch.

### Hatchery access to water, feed, or both
In the same study, water-only and feed-plus-water access in the hatcher were compared with conventional practice. Feed plus water appeared to promote feeding behavior on placement, but there were few consistent downstream benefits in measured welfare/performance outcomes; mortality was higher in both hatchery-resource groups than control, subject to the study’s design limitations. This result should not be overgeneralized to all equipment or commercial protocols. It also does not establish that chicks accessed or used the offered resources equally.

### Wet feed on farm after conventional transport
The study’s wet-feed group received wet feed for 12 hours after placement (water was available on farm); this did not consistently improve outcomes and had the lowest slaughter body weight. This is not equivalent to provision during transport, nor to hatchery feeding. The single protocol does not rule out benefits from other formulations or delivery systems.

### Feed/water during transport
This is a distinct potential intervention, not tested in the above comparison. Feasibility depends on journey duration, vehicle design, temperature, hygiene, and access to resources. Do not claim efficacy or legal requirements without direct study and jurisdiction-specific authority. A useful evidence gap is independent evaluation of actual intake, welfare indicators, and mortality under commercial transport conditions.

## Outcomes and interpretation
Prioritize individual/representative hatch-to-feed and hatch-to-water intervals, access/uptake, mortality from placement through first week and whole flock, and direct indicators of thirst/hunger or distress where validated. Record hatch-window and transport variability, not only flock means. Hydration/body-weight loss and early growth are relevant but imperfect proxies. Yolk-sac use, organ weights, FCR, and slaughter-age lesion outcomes may inform physiology or downstream health; none alone demonstrates reduced suffering. Report mortality and culls alongside survivor-only measures.

## EU/UK policy and implementation
Keep scientific recommendations separate from law. EFSA’s 2023 opinion is an evidence assessment, not legislation: its welfare recommendation to keep hatch-to-placement time no more than 48 h is stricter than transport rules that can permit chicks to travel without feed and water for longer. In England, the published meat-chicken code states that chicks arriving within 72 h of hatching and with journey time no more than 24 h need not be given feed and water in transit, but if either period is exceeded they must be; it cites the Welfare of Farmed Animals (England) Regulations 2007. EU Regulation 1/2005, Annex I, Chapter VII, section 2.1(b), contains a similar 24-hour journey / 72-hour post-hatch exception. These transport provisions do not establish that a 72-hour deprivation interval is welfare-neutral or override EFSA's scientific assessment. This note does not give legal advice; check current rules and code status in the relevant UK jurisdiction and EU Member State before operational use. The Broiler Directive concerns on-farm requirements and should not be assumed to govern hatchery practice in the same way.

Implementation comparisons should account for capital and operating costs, hatchery/farm coordination, biosecurity, egg and chick transport, staffing, environmental control, and additionality (whether the system replaces a practice or merely supplements it). Ask who bears costs and whether shifts displace production or welfare impacts elsewhere.

## Conclusion and confidence
There is strong biological plausibility and an authoritative welfare assessment that long post-hatch deprivation is undesirable, but limited comparative evidence on which commercial system yields the best net welfare. Confidence is low for ranking specific interventions on whole-life outcomes. A prudent decision framework is to measure the actual deprivation interval and welfare outcomes in the target production system, evaluate each intervention separately, and avoid treating productivity changes as a substitute for welfare evidence.
