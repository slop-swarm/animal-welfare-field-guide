# Hatch-to-first-feed and water for broiler chicks — EU/UK (draft)

**Status:** Evidence note in progress; not an endorsement of any system.  
**Decision question:** Which early-life arrangements, if any, reduce time without feed and water for broiler chicks and improve welfare, without unacceptable trade-offs? This note concerns grow-out chicks, not broiler-breeder feeding or grow-out genetics.

## Short answer
Conventional hatchery hatching followed by handling, transport, and farm placement can leave early-hatching chicks without access to feed and water for many hours. EFSA identifies this as a welfare concern, but direct evidence that any one commercial alternative improves whole-life welfare is limited. On-farm hatching removes chick transport and can provide immediate access to resources, but entails egg transport/altered farm operations and has mixed findings. Hatchery feed/water, wet feed at placement, and transport feeding are separate interventions and should not be treated as interchangeable. A controlled Northern Ireland comparison found few consistent benefits for hatchery feed/water or wet feed; in-house hatching showed some benefits and some negative findings. Do not infer that early growth or yolk-sac changes alone establish improved subjective welfare.

## Why the question matters
In conventional systems chicks hatch over a window, are processed and transported, and generally first encounter feed and water at the grow-out farm. The resulting deprivation interval varies between chicks; flock-average time from hatch is therefore an incomplete measure. EFSA’s 2023 scientific assessment concludes that a delay of more than 48 h (range 36–60 h) from hatch to first access to feed and water may lead to prolonged hunger and thirst. It recommends hatchery feeding or on-farm hatching, and keeping hatch-to-placement time as short as possible and no more than 48 h. These are expert scientific recommendations, not themselves legislation.

## Evidence on deprivation duration and outcomes
A quantitative synthesis of published aggregate results (de Jong et al., 2017; the authors call it a “meta-analysis”) found lower body weights after 24 h or more of post-hatch food/water deprivation through six weeks, and higher day-42 mortality after the 48 h category (36–60 h) than after 0 or 24 h. However, included studies used different timing origins (hatching, hatchery pull, or placement), mortality records were limited, and outcomes were mostly production/physiology rather than direct measures of subjective hunger or thirst. It supports concern about prolonged deprivation but does not cleanly identify a threshold or rank commercial systems.

A larger commercial-hatchery trial in Türkiye (19,200 chicks; 12 pens per treatment) found no significant difference in mortality or final weight for preplacement holding up to 60 h after hatchery pull under thermal-comfort conditions, but 72 h post-pull increased mortality and reduced final body weight. This is useful counter-evidence for a simple “48 h since hatch causes mortality” claim, but post-pull holding is not equivalent to hatch-to-first-access time: the clock starts later and hatch-window variation remains relevant. The trial also does not establish that the birds experienced no hunger/thirst during holding. Neither study directly tests commercial early-feeding systems in EU/UK conditions.

## Evidence by intervention

### On-farm hatching
A semi-commercial Dutch study compared on-farm (OH) and conventional hatchery hatching over three production cycles, with four rooms/cycle and one pen/treatment/room (about 1,150 birds/pen; 12 pens/treatment). OH birds had poorer day-old hock/navel scores, were heavier through day 21, and had lower total mortality; footpad dermatitis tended lower; litter dry matter was higher (but visual litter-quality scores did not differ), which the authors tentatively linked to the footpad result. Femoral-head necrosis scores were higher in OH birds at day 39 (not day 28); no dissected birds had tibial dyschondroplasia. Gait, hock burn, cleanliness, and injury did not differ. In contrast, a controlled Northern Ireland study in a commercially built broiler house randomly assigned eggs from one parent flock among five early-life treatments, with six replicate groups per treatment and around 500 birds/group. On-farm-hatched birds showed more resting and greater bursa weight, but also worse numerical chick-quality scores; there were no consistent improvements across behavioral, gait, litter, or performance outcomes. Mortality was higher for hatchery water and hatchery feed+water groups than conventional control, but not distinguishable from the on-farm group. These contrasting results do not establish a general mortality effect or net welfare winner; both studies have limited treatment-level replication and neither directly measures subjective hunger/thirst. A 2023 review reports lower first-week mortality in on-farm-hatched groups in Jessen et al. (2021) and Lingens et al. (2021; not statistically tested), but notes first-week mortality can be distorted by differences in whether second-grade chicks are removed before placement or counted among on-farm flocks and culled after placement (van den Brand et al., 2023). Other cited comparisons did not report statistically significant first-week mortality differences. The Northern Ireland authors disclosed industry funding/participation.

On-farm hatching may reduce chick handling, transport, and the post-hatch interval without resources, but requires moving eggs late in incubation and managing hatchery functions in a poultry house. Potential biosecurity, environmental-control, labor, capital, chick-quality, and hatch-window issues need independent evaluation; do not assume these are net harms or benefits without evidence. The studied protocol still included counting/grading after hatch.

### Hatchery access to water, feed, or both
In the same study, water-only and feed-plus-water access in the hatcher were compared with conventional practice. Feed plus water appeared to promote feeding behavior on placement, but there were few consistent downstream benefits in measured welfare/performance outcomes; tibial dyschondroplasia appeared worse in this group. Mortality was higher in both hatchery-resource groups than control, subject to the study’s design limitations. This result should not be overgeneralized to all equipment or commercial protocols. It also does not establish that chicks accessed or used the offered resources equally.

### Wet feed on farm after conventional transport
The study’s wet-feed group received wet feed for 12 hours after placement (water was available on farm); this did not consistently improve outcomes and had the lowest slaughter body weight. This is not equivalent to provision during transport, nor to hatchery feeding. The single protocol does not rule out benefits from other formulations or delivery systems.

### Feed/water during transport
This is a distinct potential intervention, not tested in the 2024 Northern Ireland comparison. A 2026 Canadian controlled study tested 624 Ross 308 chicks in one 4 h van journey under controlled conditions (25°C, 33% RH), with six 52-chick transport baskets per group. The feed-and-water group gained 2.47% body weight during transit while the no-resource group lost 1.71%, and had lower post-transport plasma corticosterone. These weight changes include ingesta and are not a pure measure of hydration. Mortality was lower through day 8 (0.00% vs 1.74%), but cumulative day 0–33 mortality did not differ. Later diet/pen-level replication was limited (n=3); this was one controlled transport event, not an ordinary commercial journey. The study did not assess subjective hunger/thirst. Treat as preliminary evidence, not proof of a whole-life welfare benefit. The article reports McCain Foundation funding and equipment/supplies from CBS Bio Platforms; these disclosures are relevant when weighing this single study. Feasibility depends on journey duration, vehicle design, temperature, hygiene, and safe resource access; independent commercial evaluation of actual intake and validated welfare measures remains valuable.

## Outcomes and interpretation
Prioritize individual/representative hatch-to-feed and hatch-to-water intervals, access/uptake, mortality from placement through first week and whole flock, and direct indicators of thirst/hunger or distress where validated. Record hatch-window and transport variability, not only flock means. Hydration/body-weight loss and early growth are relevant but imperfect proxies. Yolk-sac use, organ weights, FCR, and slaughter-age lesion outcomes may inform physiology or downstream health; none alone demonstrates reduced suffering. Report mortality and culls alongside survivor-only measures.

## EU/UK policy and implementation
Keep scientific recommendations separate from law. EFSA’s 2023 opinion is an evidence assessment, not legislation: its welfare recommendation to keep hatch-to-placement time no more than 48 h is stricter than transport rules that can permit chicks to travel without feed and water for longer. In England, paragraph 30 of the meat-chicken code describes the 72 h post-hatch / 24 h journey condition for feed and water during transit. The Code says its unboxed recommendations are not themselves legal text; the relevant transport exception is in Regulation 1/2005, Annex I, Chapter V, section 2.1(b), which applies in the EU and is retained in GB law. (The Code separately cites the Welfare of Farmed Animals (England) Regulations 2007 for on-farm duties.) These transport provisions do not establish that a 72-hour deprivation interval is welfare-neutral or override EFSA's scientific assessment. This note does not give legal advice; check current rules and code status in the relevant UK jurisdiction and EU Member State before operational use. The Broiler Directive concerns on-farm requirements and should not be assumed to govern hatchery practice in the same way.

Implementation comparisons should account for capital and operating costs, hatchery/farm coordination, biosecurity, egg and chick transport, staffing, environmental control, and additionality (whether the system replaces a practice or merely supplements it). Ask who bears costs and whether shifts displace production or welfare impacts elsewhere.

## Conclusion and confidence
There is strong biological plausibility and an authoritative welfare assessment that long post-hatch deprivation is undesirable, but limited comparative evidence on which commercial system yields the best net welfare. Confidence is low for ranking specific interventions on whole-life outcomes. A prudent decision framework is to measure the actual deprivation interval and welfare outcomes in the target production system, evaluate each intervention separately, and avoid treating productivity changes as a substitute for welfare evidence.
