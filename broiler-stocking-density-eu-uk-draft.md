# Broiler stocking density limits and welfare — EU/UK, reviewed 2026-10-11

**Status:** draft (built from search snippets and abstracts; full texts not yet checked)  
**One-line summary:** Lower stocking density plausibly reduces leg problems, hock/footpad lesions and fearfulness, but controlled evidence is weak for a density threshold, and field associations with footpad dermatitis (FPD) and mortality are mixed. Confidence in the size of the benefit is low.

## Scope
- Species/population: meat chickens (broilers), fast-growing strains, EU and UK houses.
- Geography and time horizon: EU Directive 2007/43/EC and national implementations; current to 2026, with much of the cited evidence 10–15+ years old.
- Relevant lifetime window: one production cycle (~35–42 days to slaughter).
- Decision-maker and decision: national regulators and producers setting maximum stocking density (kg live weight/m²).

## Proposed causal pathway
Higher live weight per m² → less space per bird for movement, litter quality deteriorates faster (wet litter → contact dermatitis) → more FPD, hock burns, leg weakness and possibly fearfulness and reduced comfort. Reducing density should reduce these, but the link is conditional on litter management, ventilation, strain and mortality.

## Evidence
| Claim / outcome | Evidence and source (date) | Strength / limitations |
|---|---|---|
| EU legal maximum 33 kg/m²; up to 39 kg/m² with extra environmental conditions; up to 42 kg/m² if cumulative daily mortality is low. Some states stricter (e.g., Austria 30, Sweden 36, Germany and UK 39). | Directive 2007/43/EC; national summaries (secondary source, date of summary not verified). | Legal fact, but secondary source; verify against the Official Journal and current UK regulation before publication. |
| Controlled trial, densities 6–56 kg/m²: leg strength fell steeply from 6 to 23 kg/m²; hock dermatitis rose 35→56 kg/m²; FPD and fearfulness only significantly higher at 56 kg/m²; no clear threshold. | Controlled study, publication date not verified (likely ~2010s). | Useful dose-response shape; single study; non-EU-standard setting; no effect on mortality or corticosterone found. |
| Dutch field study, 39–42 kg/m²: no relation between FPD and first-week or total mortality. | Dutch field study (ScienceDirect, 2019 record). | Observational; confounded by management. |
| EFSA expert elicitation: density–FPD link weak; other factors matter (regression R² ~0.25–0.33). | EFSA 2023 opinion on broiler welfare on farm (Wiley/EFSA Journal). | Expert judgement, not trial data. |
| National monitoring: largest FPD risk increase in organic systems; smaller increases in free range, Cobb strain, older birds and >39 kg/m². Lower FPD with <33 kg/m² and lower house mortality. | Longitudinal national monitoring study (PMC8185078). | Observational; density effect confounded with strain and mortality. |
| EFSA 2023 selects total mortality, wounds, carcass condemnation and FPD as useful on-farm indicators; recommends substantially reducing stocking density. | EFSA Journal 2023 (plain-language summary). | Regulatory opinion. Note: mortality is an outcome, not only a proxy. Field data link lower density with lower mortality in some studies but controlled trials find no mortality effect; treat this as unresolved.

## Expected impact and uncertainty
- Animals affected: all EU/UK broilers (order of hundreds of millions slaughtered per year in the EU; check Eurostat before citing a number).
- Magnitude/severity/duration: reductions in leg weakness and lesions over a ~5-week cycle; size unquantified. Severity of hock/foot lesions is moderate; leg weakness can be severe for affected birds.
- Probability of compliance and persistence: depends on enforcement; EU caps have been in force for years, with derogations to 42 kg/m².
- Counterfactual: producers near the cap may already comply; the marginal change is largest for those at 39–42 kg/m².
- Confidence: low-to-moderate that lower density helps birds' welfare; low on magnitude and threshold.
- Sentience/moral-weight assumptions: none needed for the within-species comparison.

## Risks, trade-offs, and alternatives
- Lower density increases cost per kg and may push production toward larger flocks or other countries with weaker rules (displacement/leakage); welfare in substitute settings must be compared.
- Density is a weak lever if litter, ventilation and strain (fast-growth genetics) are not addressed; slower-growing strains may reduce leg problems at cost of longer life per bird.

## Conclusion
Lower stocking density is probably beneficial for leg health and lesions, but the evidence does not identify a clean threshold, and the link with mortality is inconsistent. Do not infer that compliance with the 39 or 42 kg/m² caps is adequate welfare. Evidence that would change this: a recent controlled trial in EU-type housing with strain and litter controlled, and full-text verification of EFSA's density recommendations.
