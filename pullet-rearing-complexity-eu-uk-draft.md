# Can complex rearing reduce later keel fractures? — EU/UK procurement context, reviewed 2026-10-10

**Status:** draft for source review. **Summary:** Controlled longitudinal Canadian studies support a possible reduction in keel-fracture burden during lay when pullets are reared in aviary systems that enable exercise, compared with conventional cage-reared pullets. The EU/UK application is plausible but untested at commercial population scale; results do not show lower mortality, nor do they establish that “navigation training” alone causes the effect.

## Scope
- **Species/population:** commercial egg-layer pullets, hatch to transfer (~16–17 weeks), and the subsequent laying flock through end of lay.
- **Geography/time horizon:** decision context EU/UK, with underlying intervention studies from Canada; follow-up through 70 weeks of age in the cited trials.
- **Decision-maker:** egg producers and pullet suppliers specifying rearing environments and procurement clauses.
- **Decision:** whether to source pullets reared in an exercise-enabling, multi-tier aviary rather than conventional rearing cages, and which outcomes to require/monitor.

## Proposed causal pathway
A more complex pullet environment allows movement and load-bearing exercise during skeletal development; stronger bone, better motor skills, or both may reduce later fractures or injuries. These are hypotheses, not demonstrated sole mechanisms. Rearing may also introduce risks (e.g., falls/collisions) and entails management requirements. Later adult housing, strain, facility design, and handling can affect outcomes too.

## Evidence
| Claim / outcome | Evidence and source (date) | Strength / limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Keel-fracture prevalence was lower among aviary-reared pullets followed into lay in one four-flock study. | Casey-Trott et al. (2017), *Poultry Science* 96:2029–2039, https://doi.org/10.3382/ps/pex026 | Four flock replicates, 588 Lohmann Selected Leghorn-Lite pullets per flock, assigned to conventional-cage or aviary rearing; subsequently placed in conventional, 30-bird furnished, or 60-bird furnished layer cages. Fracture **prevalence** was assessed by palpation at 30, 50, 70 weeks. Prevalence increased with age; aviary-reared hens: 41.6% ± 2.8 SE vs conventional-reared: 60.3% ± 2.9 SE. Adult housing system had no significant effect on prevalence in this experiment (P=0.223). This is not fracture incidence: palpation provides a presence/absence measure, not onset timing or severity; small number of flock replicates and one strain/location limit transfer. |
| Rearing complexity and strain were associated with fracture prevalence/severity in a second experiment; only named contrasts support the claim. | Rentsch et al. (2024), *Poultry Science* 103(12):104481, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2024.104481 | Three replicate rearing flocks included brown and white strains across low-, mid-, high-complexity aviaries and conventional cages. **Only flocks 1 and 3** were followed in furnished cages during lay. At end of lay, fracture prevalence was lower for high-complexity than low-complexity aviary and conventional-cage rearing (not reported as lower than every aviary treatment). During lay, fracture severity was lower for high complexity than low and mid complexity; strain and age also affected severity. Severity assessed radiographically at 30, 50, 70 weeks, with dissection at 70 weeks; radiographic and dissection severity scores were moderately correlated (r=0.547). Multiple treatments, strains, and design features limit simple “aviary vs cage” interpretation. |
| The EU scientific assessment recommends developing pullet navigation skills. | EFSA AHAW Panel (2023), *Welfare of laying hens on farm*, https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.7789 | EU-wide evidence synthesis and expert assessment, relevant to policy/practice context; this recommendation is not itself a trial proving a procurement clause will reduce fractures in EU/UK commercial flocks. EFSA identifies bone lesions as highly relevant across housing systems. |

## Expected impact and uncertainty
The directly measured outcome is fracture prevalence and severity among sampled hens, through 70 weeks; neither paper establishes fractures prevented per flock lifetime for a typical EU/UK producer. Keel fractures are serious injuries: EFSA links them to reduced mobility and potentially pain, discomfort and distress. The studies do not provide a reliable population-level conversion from fracture prevalence to days in pain or net lifetime welfare. No mortality/culling benefit is demonstrated; neither result should be inferred from fracture measures among sampled survivors.

Confidence is moderate that rearing environment can influence later fracture burden under the studied conditions; low-to-moderate that high-complexity aviary rearing will produce a similar average effect in EU/UK supply chains. Major unknowns include strain, aviary design/complexity, adult housing, management, measurement and commercial-scale adherence. The 2017 trial held later housing treatments in its factorial design and found no adult-housing effect on prevalence; the 2024 lay follow-up used furnished cages, so it does not establish effects across all adult housing. Both are Canadian studies, not EU/UK field evaluations.

## Risks, trade-offs, and alternatives
- The trials summarized here do not establish whether aviary rearing changes mortality, culling, falls, other injuries, pecking, or production; treat these as required monitoring outcomes, not proven benefits or harms.
- Training/navigation is not isolated from exercise, bone development, strain or other components of rearing complexity. Do not claim navigation alone mediates reduced fractures.
- Record adult housing and management; the evidence does not justify ignoring later-lay conditions or using a housing label as a proxy for outcomes.
- A procurement specification could require verified rearing history, comparable keel-fracture prevalence/severity scores at transfer and during lay, mortality/culling with causes when available, and consistent sampling protocols; assess feasibility and auditability before making the clause mandatory.
- A supplier transition should account for birds displaced to other farms/systems and any welfare outcomes there. The cited experiments do not assess supply-chain displacement, costs, or adherence at scale.

## Conclusion
These studies support aviary rearing as a plausible upstream intervention for reducing keel-fracture prevalence/severity in laying hens, and EFSA provides an EU-relevant rationale for rearing that develops navigation skills. They do not establish a guaranteed EU/UK effect, an effect of navigation alone, or improvement in mortality or overall welfare across all outcomes. Producers could pilot verifiable rearing specifications with matched outcome tracking through lay before scaling; compare both injury outcomes and mortality/culling, and revisit the decision when EU/UK commercial evidence is available.
