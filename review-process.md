# Review, uncertainty, and correction process

This guide is decision support, not a certification or a substitute for a systematic review. A draft label means its claims have not yet received independent review; it does not mean the evidence is necessarily weak. Readers should not present an unreviewed note as an endorsed conclusion.

## Before review

Authors should state the decision question, population, place, time horizon, and intended audience. Separate observed results from interpretation and recommendation. Link each consequential empirical claim to the source that supports it, and describe design limitations (including confounding, selection, measurement, and generalizability) that could change the conclusion. Identify conflicts of interest and relevant expertise; lack of a conflict is not proof of neutrality.

## Independent review

For a note to be marked reviewed, seek at least one reviewer who did not write the note and can assess its subject matter or evidence methods. Ask reviewers to:

1. Check that linked sources exist and support the claims as represented; flag missing primary sources or stronger synthesis evidence.
2. Look for consequential contrary evidence, neglected outcomes, and plausible alternative explanations.
3. Test whether the conclusion follows from the evidence and stated value assumptions, including the counterfactual and trade-offs.
4. Identify uncertainty that could change a practical decision; recommend ranges or scenarios instead of unsupported precision.

Record reviewer name or chosen attribution, date, relevant expertise (if volunteered), and unresolved disagreements in the note's review history. A reviewer need not agree with the author. If review is unavailable, keep the draft label and say so plainly rather than implying review occurred. One reviewer is a minimum safeguard, not a guarantee of correctness; seek more review for high-stakes or disputed conclusions.

## Corrections and updating

Anyone may open an issue or pull request with a correction, source, or concern. Be specific: quote or link the claim, explain what is wrong or missing, and provide evidence where possible. Authors should acknowledge substantive corrections, assess whether they change the conclusion, and update the note and review history. For a material error that could mislead a decision, correct it promptly and visibly; do not silently rewrite the record. Preserve prior conclusions only when useful for understanding a changed assessment, and distinguish new evidence from correction of an earlier mistake.

Each note should have a review date or state that it has not been reviewed. Revisit it when a cited law or policy changes, a key source is corrected or retracted, materially relevant evidence appears, or the stated update trigger is met. Avoid claiming a note is current merely because its file was edited recently.

## Interpreting disagreement

Disagreement may concern facts, causal inference, expected impact, or ethical weights; identify which. Report material disagreement fairly and explain what evidence or assumptions would resolve it. Do not average incompatible views into false consensus. Keep empirical confidence distinct from moral judgments, and show when conclusions depend on contested assumptions.
