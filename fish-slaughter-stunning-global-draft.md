# How can pain and distress at slaughter be reduced for farmed fish? — draft, 2026-10-11

**Status:** draft for source review (revised 2026-10-11 after Sorrel's source-check of the WOAH PDFs; key fixes: Peeler et al. 2025 dating/scope, species-specific percussive limits, prevalence unknown, ice-time figure, recommendation vs Code requirement). **One-line summary:** Global prevalence of stunning in farmed-fish slaughter is unknown, and killing methods in common use (asphyxia in air or ice, CO₂ narcosis, gill cutting without stunning) cause distress during delays to loss of consciousness; electrical stunning, and percussive stunning for species where it is effective (mainly salmon/trout), can render fish insensible quickly, but efficacy is species-specific and verification of stun outcomes remains a major unresolved problem.

## Scope
- Species/population and production context: farmed finfish across species groups (salmonids, carp, sea bass/sea bream, tilapia, eel, catfish); harvested for human consumption. Fish are farmed in very diverse systems (sea cages, ponds, tanks, raceways, ponds drained for harvest).
- Geography and time horizon: global, with emphasis on EU/UK where statutory and review evidence is strongest; the note is current as of 2026-10-11.
- Relevant lifetime window: pre-slaughter handling (fasting, crowding, pumping, transport) and the killing process itself; minutes to hours, experienced by every fish harvested.
- Decision-maker and decision: producers, retailers and certifiers choosing harvest and stunning methods; whether to require pre-slaughter stunning and to verify it.

## Proposed causal pathway
Fish are sentient and capable of experiencing pain, fear and stress (consensus reviews cited below). Killing methods such as asphyxia in air or ice slurry, CO₂ narcosis, and gill cutting without prior stunning leave the fish conscious while experiencing severe distress — up to 22 minutes reported in ice (Peeler et al. 2025, p.18) and longer in air for some species such as eels — though duration is method- and species-specific. Pre-stunning (electrical, or percussive for species where it works) is intended to induce immediate loss of consciousness until death, thereby removing that experience. The pathway can fail if stunning parameters are wrong for the species, if fish are not correctly positioned (a known failure mode in electrical systems), or if fish recover consciousness before death.

## Evidence

Note on sources: the WOAH-commissioned scientific review (Peeler et al., dated 26 June 2025 on the cover; hosted at a Feb-2026 path) covers only major groups — salmonids, cyprinids, tilapines — not the full species list in the scope above, and its findings are largely method- and species-specific. Claims below are narrowed accordingly; where a claim rests on expert recommendation rather than measured outcomes, that is stated.

**Scale context (uncertain):** farmed finfish killed annually plausibly number in the tens of billions by individual count (an unverified order-of-magnitude hypothesis; fish-count estimates have wide uncertainty because of heterogeneous species sizes). Every harvested fish passes through pre-slaughter handling and killing, so per-animal severity here applies to a very large population — though species, system and region strongly condition which methods are actually used.

## Expected impact and uncertainty
- Animals affected: all fish harvested by a producer or supply chain that adopts stunning; very large populations per intervention site.
- Magnitude, severity and duration: removal of distress during unconsciousness-delaying kill methods — minutes in ice (up to 22 min reported), longer in air for some species — per fish at harvest. Which methods prevail in which systems is largely unknown at global scale.
- Probability of adoption: a hypothesis, not a measured rate — plausibly higher in EU salmon/trout (where commercial stunning systems exist and reviews are recent), lower where infrastructure is absent (pond harvest, small-scale systems) and for species resistant to percussive stunning. Adoption often follows retailer/certifier requirements rather than regulation; no prevalence data were found to support a specific probability.
- Counterfactual: if production shifts to regions or systems without stunning (e.g., market substitution away from high-welfare supply chains), the improvement is displaced rather than realised—check where fish go when procurement changes.
- Confidence: moderate that electrical stunning reduces suffering at killing for well-studied species (salmonids, sea bass/bream); low on efficacy for less-studied species (tilapia, catfish, carp, eel) and on current global prevalence and trend.
- Sentience / moral-weight assumptions: the note assumes fish experience pain and distress (supported by the cited reviews). Readers weighting fish far below mammals should test whether the conclusion changes: because the affected population is enormous and the insult is severe and universal, this conclusion is relatively robust to wide ranges of relative moral weight.

## Risks, trade-offs, and alternatives
- Electrical stunning can fail silently if fish are misaligned or parameters are wrong; a poorly verified "stunned" process may be no better than no stunning. Verification of insensibility (behavioural and physiological checks) is essential, not optional.
- Pre-slaughter handling itself (crowding, pumping, poor water quality) causes hypoxia, stress and trauma and is not solved by stunning; improvements must not be limited to the kill step.
- Equipment cost may concentrate production; where investment is unaffordable, alternative low-cost improvements (shorter air exposure, immediate percussive spiking/coring by trained staff) should be considered against doing nothing.
- Reducing meat demand or shifting consumption is an alternative lever that avoids slaughter entirely but substitutes other considerations this note does not evaluate.

## Conclusion
For well-studied species (salmonids in particular), evidence supports stunning before killing (electrical, or percussive where species-appropriate, with species-specific parameters and verified insensibility) over asphyxia, CO₂ narcosis or gill cutting without stunning. For species resistant to percussive stunning (catfish, pangasius, carp, tilapia per Peeler et al. 2025) and for less-studied taxa, the case rests on expert recommendation rather than measured species-specific outcomes. It should not be inferred that stunning is widely practised globally, that any system labelled "stunning" is effective, or that pre-slaughter handling welfare is covered by the intervention. Evidence that would change the conclusion: robust verification data showing commercial systems reliably render the target species insensible, or new evidence that currently-used methods achieve equivalent loss of consciousness.
