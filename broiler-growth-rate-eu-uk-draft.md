# Should procurement specify broiler growth rate? — EU/UK grow-out, draft, 2026-10-10

**Status:** draft for source review. **Summary:** Evidence supports treating realized growth rate as an auditable procurement measure alongside strain identity, not treating “slow-growing” as a universal breed label. Several comparative studies associate slower-growing genotypes/systems with improved locomotion or behavioral outcomes, but production trade-offs and inconsistent/null endpoints matter; evidence does not establish 50 g/day as a biological welfare threshold or growth rate as the sole cause.

## Scope
- **Species/population:** commercial meat chickens from placement/hatch through slaughter; exclude breeder flocks and hatchery welfare except where needed to define population.
- **Geography/time horizon:** procurement decisions for EU/UK supply; evidence includes UK commercial farms and controlled comparisons in other settings. Outcomes across one grow-out cycle, with slaughter age and weight reported.
- **Decision-maker and decision:** retailers, food-service buyers, and suppliers deciding whether to specify an auditable average daily gain (ADG) ceiling rather than rely on strain/breed labels alone, and which welfare and production outcomes to monitor.
- **Question:** Does selecting for a measured lower growth rate, compared with conventional fast-growing commercial strains, improve broiler welfare enough to justify procurement requirements, and what trade-offs or safeguards should accompany such a requirement?

## Proposed causal pathway
Genetic selection for slower growth may reduce rapid weight gain and associated cardiopulmonary/locomotor burden, allowing more activity and better mobility. But a “slow” label is not itself an animal outcome: realized growth, genotype, sex, diet, stocking density, housing, health management, and slaughter age can interact. A standard should therefore define measurement and monitor direct animal outcomes; a growth-rate target is an intervention proxy, not proof of improved welfare.

## Evidence

| A numerical benchmark can make procurement more auditable, but it is not a legal or proven biological cutoff. | EFSA AHAW Panel, *Welfare of broilers on farm* (2023), https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.7788 | EFSA uses <50 g/day to describe slower-growing broilers in its assessment and recommends a maximum growth rate of 50 g/day. This is a scientific recommendation/assessment benchmark, **not an EU/UK legal requirement**, universal breed definition, or demonstrated threshold below which welfare is assured. |
| Direct comparative research finds welfare differences between commercial faster- and slower-growing strains, but effects are outcome-specific. | Dixon et al. At assessments as birds approached 2.2 and 2.5 kg (so slower birds were older), JA757 had better gait, hock-burn, feather-cover and breast-cleanliness scores; footpad dermatitis was infrequent and did not differ by strain. Weekly behavior observations also found more standing, moving, foraging, preening, dustbathing and perching in JA757, with locomotion decreasing with age. Direct comparison is informative, but strain and growth rate are not separable; age-at-weight and study conditions limit causal/general claims. |
| Farm comparisons suggest slower-growing commercial systems can differ on behavior, health, and production, while leaving room for system confounding. | Rayner et al. Fast/high-density Condition 4 had the highest gait scores, poorest footpad/hock health, higher mortality and fewer observed positive-behavior indicators (including play and foraging) than Conditions 1–3. This is relevant commercial evidence, but breed and density co-varied; Condition 4 does not isolate growth rate. |
| UK commercial production data show production and welfare contrasts, but this is not a randomized growth-rate intervention. | Baxter et al. ### How to make a growth-rate clause auditable
Specify the calculation and data fields rather than only a label. At minimum report flock-level ADG as live-weight gain divided by elapsed age (g/bird/day), with placement/hatch age and slaughter age, mean slaughter weight, and the underlying weight records/measurement method stated. Report flock distributions or range as well as mean, and stratify by sex when known; identify strain, farm, and cycle. Define whether the clock begins at hatch or placement and how mortality/culls and partial depopulation are handled. A threshold without a common protocol may not compare like with like. The evidence here does not establish that any one measurement protocol or 50 g/day cutoff guarantees a welfare benefit.

### Outcomes and trade-offs
Evaluate animal outcomes distinctly from the procurement proxy and production measures: gait/locomotion and mobility; activity and behavior; mortality and culling (with causes where available); and footpad/hock lesions or other injury/health measures. Report assessment ages, denominator, and severity/prevalence, including deaths/culls rather than survivor-only results. Separately report slaughter age, final weight, feed efficiency, and yield where available. The longer grow-out and efficiency differences can affect cost and resource use; neither those costs nor environmental impacts determine the welfare conclusion by themselves. Null or non-improving endpoints must remain visible.

## Expected impact and uncertainty
The evidence is sufficient for a useful note and for piloting auditable purchasing criteria, not for a definitive ranking of all hybrids or a causal claim that growth rate alone explains welfare. Direct strain comparisons support plausible welfare effects, while farm-based evidence helps assess commercial relevance but is more confounded. Certainty is lower for transfer to all EU/UK systems and for a particular numerical procurement threshold. Key uncertainties include genotype-by-management interactions, farm-level implementation, sex and slaughter age, measurement comparability, and whether gains in locomotion/behavior coincide with changes in mortality, lesions, and other serious harms. No population-wide number of animals benefited or days of suffering avoided can be estimated from these studies alone.

## Risks, trade-offs, and alternatives
- Do not assume a slower-growth label guarantees good welfare, nor that a growth cap alone addresses stocking density, litter, health care, or slaughter.
- Longer grow-out may entail production/efficiency trade-offs; evaluate separately and avoid shifting animals to less visible supply chains or other systems without checking welfare there.
- Procurement should consider a phased pilot with flock-level growth records and repeated welfare assessments, comparing matched management where possible. A label-only standard risks rewarding nominal classification rather than measured outcomes.
- Do not infer breeder-flock effects: breeder populations are outside this note’s scope and have distinct welfare questions.

## Conclusion
For EU/UK buyers, specifying measured growth rate alongside strain identity is more transparent and potentially more meaningful than relying on breed labels alone. EFSA’s 50 g/day recommendation is a useful scientific reference point, not law or a validated welfare threshold. Existing evidence supports a qualified concern that faster growth can compromise some welfare outcomes, but findings vary and commercial comparisons have confounding and limited observation coverage. Any procurement clause should define ADG measurement, disclose flock/sex/age/weight data, track direct welfare outcomes and production trade-offs separately, and be evaluated in context before broad claims or rankings are made.
